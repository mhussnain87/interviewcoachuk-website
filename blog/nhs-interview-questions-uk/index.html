What to expect at an NHS interview

NHS interviews are unusually predictable, and that is good news. Unlike a private-sector interview where the format shifts with whoever is hiring, NHS recruitment follows a structured process designed to be fair and auditable. Every candidate for a post is asked broadly the same questions, and every answer is scored against a matrix agreed before anyone walked into the room.

Most NHS interviews share these features:

  • A panel, not one interviewer. Usually two or three people: the recruiting manager, someone from the wider team, and often a service user representative or someone from another department for larger posts.
  • Six to eight competency questions. Nearly all begin "Tell me about a time when" or "Describe a situation where". They want a real example, not a hypothetical.
  • 45 to 60 minutes. Band 5 posts are often shorter; Band 7 and above run longer and frequently add a presentation.
  • Scoring as you speak. Panel members write while you answer. This feels unnerving and is entirely normal — they are marking against criteria, not judging you.
  • Questions rooted in NHS values. Care, compassion and respect are not decoration. They are literally on the scoring sheet.

The single most common mistake is preparing what you want to say about yourself rather than preparing evidence. The panel cannot award marks for enthusiasm. They award marks for specific things you have actually done.

How NHS interviews are scored

Understanding the scoring system changes how you answer, and almost nobody looks it up before they go in.

Most trusts score each question on a scale of 0 to 5:

  • 0 — no evidence. The candidate did not answer the question asked.
  • 1 — poor. A vague or hypothetical answer with no real example.
  • 2 — limited. An example, but thin on detail or on the candidate's own contribution.
  • 3 — satisfactory. A relevant example with a clear action and outcome. This is usually the appointable threshold.
  • 4 — good. A strong example with reflection on what was learned.
  • 5 — excellent. A compelling example that also evidences NHS values and wider service impact.

Three practical consequences follow from this, and they are worth more than any individual model answer:

Every question is scored separately. A brilliant answer to question three cannot rescue a blank on question five. If you genuinely have no example, say so and offer the closest thing you do have rather than going silent.

Detail is what separates a 2 from a 4. "I supported a colleague who was struggling" scores low. "I noticed a Band 5 was staying late three shifts running, asked her for a coffee off the ward, and found she was covering two bays because of sickness" scores high. Same story, very different marks.

Say "I", not "we". Panels cannot score a team. If your answer is entirely in the first-person plural, the marker has nothing of yours to award points for. Describe the team context, then be specific about your own actions.

Some trusts also weight questions, so a safeguarding question in a clinical role may be worth double. You will rarely be told this in advance, which is a good reason to treat every question as though it counts most.

The 25 most common NHS interview questions

These are the questions that come up most often across NHS bands and roles, with guidance on what the panel is actually marking. Prepare an example for each theme rather than memorising a script — panels can tell, and a rehearsed answer that does not quite fit the question scores worse than an honest one that does.

1. Why do you want to work for the NHS?

Almost always the opener, and almost always answered badly. "I want to help people" is true of every candidate and scores nothing. The panel is testing whether you understand what you are joining.

What raises the score: a specific reason tied to this service and this trust. Something you have read in their strategy, a service they run that interests you, or a genuine experience that pointed you here. If you have been a patient or a carer, that is legitimate and often powerful, provided you connect it to what you would bring rather than only what you felt.

2. Tell me about a time you provided excellent patient care

The core clinical question. Panels want the small, specific thing you did that went beyond the task.

What raises the score: choose an example where you noticed something nobody asked you to notice. Sitting with a distressed relative, spotting that a patient had not eaten, adapting communication for someone with a hearing impairment. Then say what changed as a result.

3. How do you handle a distressed or aggressive patient?

This is a safety question disguised as a communication question. They are checking you de-escalate rather than either freeze or escalate.

What raises the score: naming your actual approach — lowering your voice, giving space, removing an audience, checking for unmet needs like pain or confusion — and being clear about when you would call for help. Saying you would manage entirely alone is a red flag, not a strength.

4. Describe a time you worked as part of a team

Easy to answer, hard to score well on, because most people describe the team rather than themselves.

What raises the score: a clear account of your specific role, and ideally a moment where you adapted for someone else — picked up a task, changed how you communicated, or supported a colleague who was struggling.

5. Tell me about a time you disagreed with a colleague

They are not looking for someone who never disagrees. They are looking for someone who disagrees professionally.

What raises the score: a real disagreement, handled directly and privately, with the patient or service at the centre of your reasoning. Show that you listened, and be honest if you turned out to be wrong. Candidates who claim they always turn out to be right score lower than those who reflect.

6. How do you prioritise when everything is urgent?

A universal NHS question because it is a universal NHS reality.

What raises the score: an actual method — clinical need first, then deadlines, then what can be safely delegated or delayed — plus evidence that you escalate early rather than silently drowning. Mentioning that you told someone you were struggling is a strength, not a weakness.

7. What does equality, diversity and inclusion mean to you in practice?

Often scored against a specific criterion, and often answered in generalities.

What raises the score: the phrase "in practice" is the whole question. Give an example: arranging an interpreter, adapting information for someone with a learning disability, challenging a comment from a colleague, noticing that a service was harder to access for one group.

8. Tell me about a time you made a mistake

The question candidates most want to dodge. Dodging it scores zero.

What raises the score: a real mistake, reported promptly, with the focus on what you did next — who you told, how you mitigated harm, what changed afterwards. The NHS runs on a duty of candour, so a candidate who conceals errors is a genuine risk. Choose something real but not catastrophic.

9. How would you handle a safeguarding concern?

In clinical and many non-clinical NHS roles this is close to compulsory, and it is often weighted heavily.

What raises the score: knowing that your job is to recognise and report, not to investigate. Name the route — safeguarding lead, your line manager, the trust's policy — say that you document factually, and be clear you would escalate even if unsure. "I would raise it even if I turned out to be wrong" is exactly what they want to hear.

10. Tell me about a time you had to advocate for a patient

Tests whether you will speak up when it is uncomfortable.

What raises the score: an example where you pushed for something on a patient's behalf — a review, an adjustment, a delay to a discharge that was not safe — and did so through the proper channel rather than by confrontation.

11. How do you keep your knowledge and practice up to date?

A revalidation-flavoured question for clinical roles, a development question for everyone else.

What raises the score: specifics. A named course, a journal, a community of practice, supervision, reflective practice. Vague claims to "keep on top of things" score nothing.

12. Describe a service improvement you have contributed to

Increasingly common from Band 5 upwards, and the question most candidates are least ready for.

What raises the score: it does not have to be a formal project. Changing how a handover ran, redesigning a form, reducing a duplicate step. State the problem, what you changed, and what improved — ideally with a number, even a rough one.

13. How do you manage stress and your own wellbeing?

Asked genuinely. NHS turnover is expensive and panels are wary of candidates who will burn out.

What raises the score: honest, practical strategies plus evidence you use support rather than absorbing everything. Claiming never to find the work stressful reads as either untrue or unaware.

14. Tell me about a time you dealt with a complaint

Relevant to clinical and administrative roles alike.

What raises the score: listening first, not defending. Acknowledging the person's experience, explaining what you could and could not do, escalating appropriately, and following up. Complaints handled well often end better than the original service.

15. How would you support a colleague who was struggling?

Tests compassion toward staff, not just patients — an explicit NHS People Promise theme.

What raises the score: noticing early, approaching privately, offering something concrete, and knowing when to involve a manager or occupational health rather than carrying it alone.

16. Tell me about a time you had to work under significant pressure

Close cousin of the prioritisation question, but here they want the emotional dimension too.

What raises the score: a specific pressured period, what you actually did to stay effective, and honesty about what was hard. Reflection scores.

17. What do you know about our trust?

Pure preparation marks, freely available to anyone who does ten minutes of reading.

What raises the score: their CQC rating and what they are working on, a service or specialism they are known for, something from their current strategy, and why that makes you want to work there specifically rather than at any NHS organisation.

18. How do you handle confidential information?

An information-governance question, especially for administrative and corporate roles.

What raises the score: practical discipline — not discussing patients in corridors or lifts, locking screens, sharing on a need-to-know basis, following Caldicott principles — and knowing how to report a breach if one happens.

19. Tell me about a time you adapted to a significant change

The NHS reorganises constantly. They want to know you cope.

What raises the score: a change you did not choose, your honest initial reaction, and what you did to make it work. Candidates who claim to love all change score lower than those who acknowledge difficulty and adapt anyway.

20. How would you deal with a colleague whose practice concerned you?

A whistleblowing and patient-safety question. Handled carefully, it is a strong scoring opportunity.

What raises the score: patient safety first, raise it through the proper route, document, and escalate if not addressed. Reference Freedom to Speak Up if you know it. Saying you would ignore it to keep the peace fails the question outright.

21. What are your strengths and weaknesses?

Still asked, despite everything.

What raises the score: a strength backed by evidence rather than an adjective, and a genuine weakness with active mitigation. The fake weakness — "I care too much" — is transparent and costs you marks.

22. How do you communicate with someone who has additional needs?

Tests both accessibility awareness and practical skill.

What raises the score: concrete adaptations — interpreters, easy-read materials, involving a carer appropriately, checking understanding rather than assuming, giving more time — and an example of doing it.

23. Where do you see yourself in five years?

Partly a retention question. They are investing in you.

What raises the score: ambition that makes sense within the NHS and ideally within their organisation. Development, specialism, possibly progression. Answers implying you will leave quickly, or that you have no thoughts at all, both score poorly.

24. Tell me about a time you went above and beyond

An invitation to show values in action.

What raises the score: something small and human usually beats something heroic. Staying with someone who was frightened, chasing a result nobody had followed up, noticing a detail in a patient's notes that others had missed.

25. Do you have any questions for us?

Not a formality. Many panels score it, and "no thank you" is the most common answer — which makes it the easiest place to stand out.

What raises the score: two or three prepared questions about the role, the team or the service. Strong options include what success looks like in the first six months, what the team's biggest current challenge is, and whether the panel has any reservations about your application that you could address now. That last one gives you a chance to fix a doubt before you leave the room.

NHS interview questions by band

The questions above appear at every level, but what the panel expects from your answers changes considerably as you move up the Agenda for Change bands. The same question about prioritisation gets a different mark at Band 5 and Band 7, because the scope of the answer should be different.

Band 5 panels want safe, competent practice and self-awareness. They expect you to know your limits and escalate appropriately. Examples from training or placements are entirely acceptable.

Band 6 is where leadership enters. You are expected to have supervised or supported others, contributed to service improvement, and managed situations rather than only responded to them. Answers that stay purely at the level of your own caseload tend to score as Band 5 answers.

Band 7 expects strategic thinking, accountability for a team or service, and awareness of the wider NHS context — governance, budgets, the pressures your service sits inside. Panels look for someone who thinks about the system, not only the shift.

NHS interview questions by role

Alongside the band, your profession shapes what comes up. A midwifery panel and a healthcare assistant panel are both scoring NHS values, but the scenarios they use are entirely different.

  • Healthcare assistant interview questions — personal care, dignity, working under supervision
  • Midwife interview questions — continuity of care, escalation, advocacy in labour
  • Paramedic interview questions — autonomous decisions, scene management, handover
  • Social worker interview questions — safeguarding, capacity, multi-agency working

For non-clinical NHS roles — administration, finance, HR, business support — the competency framework is identical and the values questions are the same. Your examples come from service users, colleagues and process rather than from patients, and questions on confidentiality, data handling and working across departments carry more weight.

NHS interview presentations

From Band 6 upwards, and for most management and corporate posts, you will often be asked to prepare a presentation. This catches people out because the brief arrives with the invitation and is easy to underestimate.

Typical NHS presentation topics include:

  • How would you improve a named service or pathway?
  • What are the biggest challenges facing this service over the next three years?
  • How would you lead your team through a period of change?
  • What does quality mean in this role, and how would you measure it?
  • Present a service improvement you have led and what you learned from it.
  • How would you use data to improve patient outcomes in this area?

Most briefs allow five to ten minutes with a short question period afterwards. The commonest failures are straightforward and avoidable:

Overrunning. Panels stop you at time, mid-sentence, and you lose whatever was in your conclusion. Rehearse aloud and cut until you finish comfortably inside the limit.

Too many slides. Five or six is plenty for ten minutes. Dense slides that you then read aloud score badly on communication.

Not answering the brief. If they asked how you would improve the service, the panel needs a proposal, not a description of the current state. Re-read the brief the morning of the interview and check every slide earns its place against it.

No evidence. Reference something real — a national priority, the trust's own strategy, a metric the service reports. It demonstrates you have looked outward.

Bring printed copies for each panel member. It is a small thing that consistently lands well, and it saves you if the technology fails.

How to structure your answers using STAR

Every NHS competency question is scored more easily when your answer has a shape. STAR gives it one:

  • Situation — where you were and what was happening. Two sentences. Enough context to make the rest make sense, no more.
  • Task — what you specifically were responsible for. This is where "we" quietly becomes "I".
  • Action — what you did, step by step. This should be the longest part by some distance, because it is the part carrying the marks.
  • Result — what happened, and what you learned or changed afterwards.

The commonest error is spending two minutes on the situation and twenty seconds on the action. Panels cannot score background. If you find yourself still explaining the ward layout a minute in, move on.

The second commonest error is stopping at the result. Adding one sentence of reflection — what you would do differently, what the team changed as a result — is often what lifts an answer from a 3 to a 4.

Prepare six stories, not twenty-five answers. A well-chosen example usually covers several competencies: a single incident might evidence teamwork, escalation, communication and patient safety all at once. Six flexible stories will cover almost any NHS interview, and they are far easier to recall under pressure than a memorised script per question.

The six NHS values and how to evidence them

The NHS Constitution sets out six values, and they appear on scoring matrices across the service. Knowing them is worth little; having an example ready for each is worth a great deal.

Working together for patients. Evidence: a time you worked across teams or professions to sort something out for a patient.

Respect and dignity. Evidence: a time you protected someone's privacy or preferences when it would have been easier not to.

Commitment to quality of care. Evidence: a time you raised a standard, questioned a practice, or improved something small.

Compassion. Evidence: a time you responded to distress — a patient's or a colleague's — rather than only to the task.

Improving lives. Evidence: a time your work changed an outcome, even modestly.

Everyone counts. Evidence: a time you made a service work for someone it was not designed for.

You do not need to name the value aloud, though it does no harm. What matters is that when the panel looks down at the criterion, your answer has given them something to tick.

Tips for the day

  • Take notes in with you. Most NHS panels allow it and many expect it. A single page listing your six stories by keyword is enough to stop your mind going blank.
  • Ask for the question again if you need it. Entirely acceptable, and far better than answering a question nobody asked.
  • Pause before answering. Three seconds of silence feels enormous to you and normal to them. It reliably produces a better-structured answer.
  • Watch your pronouns. If you hear yourself saying "we" repeatedly, correct course to what you personally did.
  • Read the job description and person specification again that morning. The competencies being scored are usually listed there almost verbatim.
  • Know your route and arrive early. Hospital sites are large, parking is genuinely difficult, and arriving flustered costs you the first question.
  • Have your questions ready. Two or three, written down. See question 25 above.

Key takeaways

  • NHS interviews are competency-based and scored against a matrix — usually 0 to 5 per question, with 3 as the appointable threshold.
  • Detail and first-person ownership are what separate a low score from a high one on the same story.
  • Prepare six flexible STAR stories rather than a scripted answer for every question.
  • Have an example ready for each of the six NHS Constitution values.
  • From Band 6 upward, expect a presentation and treat the brief as the marking scheme.
  • Always have questions to ask at the end — it is frequently scored.

NHS interview FAQ

How long does an NHS interview last?

Most NHS interviews run 45 to 60 minutes. Band 5 posts are often closer to 30 to 45 minutes with six or seven questions. Band 7 and above frequently last longer and include a presentation, and senior posts may add a second panel or stakeholder session on the same day.

What is the NHS interview scoring system?

Most trusts score each question from 0 to 5, where 0 means no evidence and 5 means an excellent answer that also demonstrates NHS values. An average of around 3 is typically the appointable threshold. Every question is scored independently, so a strong answer cannot compensate for one you left blank, and some trusts weight safety-critical questions more heavily.

What should I wear to an NHS interview?

Smart business dress is the safe choice for every NHS interview, clinical or corporate. You are not expected to wear a suit for a healthcare assistant post, but tidy, professional and comfortable matters. If any part of the day involves a clinical area, make sure you can meet bare-below-the-elbows requirements.

What presentation topics come up in NHS interviews?

Common briefs ask how you would improve a named service, the biggest challenges facing the service over the next few years, how you would lead a team through change, or a service improvement you have delivered. Most allow five to ten minutes. Answer the brief precisely, keep to five or six slides, rehearse to time, and bring printed copies for the panel.

Are NHS interview questions the same for every band?

The questions are broadly similar, but the expected depth is not. Band 5 answers should show safe practice and appropriate escalation. Band 6 answers need leadership and service improvement. Band 7 answers need strategic thinking and accountability for a team or service. Giving a Band 5 answer at a Band 7 interview is one of the most common reasons strong clinicians do not get appointed.

Can I take notes into an NHS interview?

Usually yes, and many panels expect it. A single page of prompts — your six STAR stories by keyword, plus the questions you want to ask — is sensible preparation, not a sign of weakness. Read from it sparingly rather than reciting.

What if I do not have an example for a question?

Say so, then offer the closest thing you do have. A transferable example from training, a placement, a previous sector or a voluntary role will score far better than silence or an invented story. Panels are experienced at spotting fabricated examples, and the follow-up questions usually expose them.
