Police officer interviews are genuinely different from most job interviews — and a lot of candidates underestimate that.

The format is structured and competency-based, drawn from the Policing Professional Framework. Panels aren't just listening to what you did. They're listening for evidence of the College of Policing's core values: integrity, public service, respect, transparency.

That means a vague answer about "working well in a team" won't land the way you think it will. You need a specific situation, your specific actions, and a clear outcome — STAR method, properly applied.

Questions like "Give an example of when you showed integrity under pressure" or "Describe how you adapted your communication for a different audience" are far more common than candidates expect.

One tip that makes a real difference in police officer competency interviews:

Don't just describe what happened — describe *why you made the choices you made*.

Panels are trained assessors. When they ask "Tell me about a time you dealt with a confrontational person," they're not grading your story. They're grading your judgement, your values, and your self-awareness.

So after your example, always reflect briefly. Something like: "I chose that approach because I wanted to de-escalate without undermining their dignity." That one sentence signals exactly what they're looking for — respect, proportionality, emotional intelligence.

It sounds simple, but most candidates stop at the outcome and forget the reasoning. The reasoning is often what separates a pass from a strong pass.

STAR questions feel daunting but they're actually quite learnable once you see the structure clearly — you've got this.

Here's what helps:

Start by building a shortlist of five or six real experiences from your life — work, volunteering, sport, anything. Label what each one demonstrates (handling conflict, working under pressure, ethical decision, teamwork, etc.). Most STAR questions in police interviews map to one of those themes.

When you're in the room, resist the urge to rush. Take a breath, set the scene briefly, then focus most of your answer on *what you actually did and why* — that's what assessors are scoring.

The "why" matters especially in policing interviews because they're assessing values, not just competence.
